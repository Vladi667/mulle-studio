Web agency vs freelancer in Switzerland: choosing the right provider structure

The question of web agency vs freelancer in Switzerland is almost always framed as a choice between two options, and that framing is the reason it stays difficult to answer. The decision is not really about price, and it is not about talent either, since strong and weak practitioners exist on both sides. It is about structure: what a provider is built to carry, what it therefore has to charge for, who actually sits at the keyboard, and what happens to the project when that person is unavailable. This guide sets out the three structures available in Switzerland, what a budget finances inside each one, how each bills, the risks of each stated evenly, and a checklist of questions that will tell a buyer more in ten minutes than a proposal document will in ten pages.

The choice is not between a cheap option and a safe one. It is between three different cost structures, each of which finances something different, and each of which fails in a different way.

There are three models on the Swiss market, not two

The comparison is usually set up as a freelancer on one side and an agency on the other. In practice a Swiss buyer commissioning a website or a brand identity is choosing between three distinct structures, and the third one is frequently the closest fit for a small business or a founder-led company. Naming it changes the shape of the decision, because a buyer who believes there are only two options tends to choose the one whose weaknesses are easier to see.

The freelancer

A freelancer is an individual selling their own time, usually with one clear specialism: design, front-end development, WordPress, Webflow, copywriting, or SEO. The engagement is direct. The person who answers the first email is the person doing the work. Because there is no structure to fund beyond the individual, the rate reflects execution time and very little else. Scope is typically defined by the buyer, and the freelancer executes it. Where a project needs a discipline outside the freelancer's specialism, that gap is either subcontracted, handed back to the client, or left open.

The agency

An agency is a team plus the structure that lets a team function. That structure is real work and it has a real cost: an account or project manager who holds the relationship, a producer or lead who coordinates between disciplines, premises, employment costs, insurance, business development, and the internal time spent briefing people who were not in the room when the client spoke. The upside is coverage and continuity. Several disciplines are available under one roof, holidays and illness do not stop the project, and there is a documented process that does not live in one person's head.

The independent studio

Between the two sits a structure that is neither a generalist freelancer nor an agency: one practitioner, or a very small operation, running an agency-standard process without the commercial layer that a team requires. The distinguishing features are usually a defined method rather than an open-ended hourly arrangement, published or fixed prices, and delivery of the finished assets rather than access to a platform. The single point of contact is also the person executing. The trade is straightforward: the buyer gets agency-level care without paying for coordination, and accepts that capacity is genuinely limited.

None of these is the correct answer in general. Each is the correct answer for a particular situation, and the rest of this page is about identifying which situation applies. A buyer who identifies the situation first will usually find that the structure follows from it without much argument.

What a budget actually finances in each model

The most useful way to compare quotes across structures is not to compare the totals. It is to ask what each total is paying for. Two figures that look similar can finance very different things, and a figure that looks high may be buying coordination and continuity that the project genuinely needs. A figure that looks low may simply have less inside it.

How each model bills, and what that transfers to whom

Billing structure is not an administrative detail. It determines who carries the risk that the project takes longer than anyone expected, and that risk is the single biggest variable in a web or identity project. Three arrangements cover almost everything on offer in Switzerland, and each one moves that risk to a different party.

Hourly or daily rates

Hourly and daily billing is common across freelancers and agencies alike, with agency hourly rates observed on the Swiss market broadly in the CHF 120 to CHF 180 band. Under this structure the client carries the estimation risk. If the build takes 40 hours more than the estimate, the client pays for 40 more hours. This is genuinely the fairest arrangement when scope cannot be known in advance, which is the case for open-ended platform work, ongoing development, or a project whose requirements will be discovered as it proceeds. It is a poor fit for a defined deliverable, because the buyer has no ceiling.

Fixed price

A fixed price transfers the estimation risk to the provider. If the work runs long, the provider absorbs it. This is only possible where scope is genuinely defined in advance, which is why fixed pricing appears most often on deliverables with a repeatable shape: a brand identity, a brochure site, a standard e-commerce build. The thing to verify is not whether the price is fixed, but what it is fixed against. A fixed price attached to a vague scope is not a fixed price; it is a deposit against a negotiation that has not happened yet. The two questions that settle it are what triggers a variation, and what a variation costs.

Monthly retainer

A retainer buys availability and continuity rather than a deliverable. It suits work that is genuinely continuous: marketing, ongoing content, maintenance, performance work, or an evolving product. It is the wrong instrument for a one-off build, and it becomes an expensive habit when it outlives the work it was set up to cover. A retainer should have a stated scope and a stated exit, in the same way a fixed-price project has a stated deliverable. If the scope of a monthly arrangement cannot be written down, the arrangement is buying access rather than output, and the buyer should decide deliberately whether access is what is wanted.

Fritz works on fixed prices for one-off deliverables, with the brand identity published at CHF 500. That figure sits at the very bottom of the range observed on the Swiss market for a full brand identity, CHF 1'200 to CHF 3'500. The remaining figures, one-off and monthly, are set out on the pricing page rather than restated here. What matters structurally is that they are published rather than quoted case by case: the estimation risk sits with the studio, and a buyer can calibrate before making contact.

The real risks of each model, stated evenly

Every structure has a characteristic way of going wrong. Knowing which failure a model is prone to is more useful than knowing its selling points, because the selling points are already in the proposal. The three lists below are set out in the same terms and none of them is softened.

Risks of hiring a freelancer

  • Continuity. One person means no cover. Illness, an overrun on another project, or a change of career direction can stall the work, and there is no second person to pick it up.
  • Single point of failure on access. If domain registration, hosting, analytics and the repository all sit in one individual's accounts, an unplanned absence becomes an operational problem and not just a scheduling one.
  • Uneven scope. A specialist covers their specialism well. The parts outside it (content, technical SEO, accessibility, post-launch handling) may be assumed by the client to be included when they were never quoted.
  • Variance in method. Freelance practice ranges from highly systematic to entirely improvised, and the difference is not visible in a portfolio. Portfolios show outcomes, not process.

Risks of hiring an agency

  • Paying for structure the project does not need. Coordination, account management and premises are real costs that a team requires in order to work. On a small, well-defined project they can represent a meaningful share of a budget that would otherwise go into execution.
  • Distance between the sale and the build. In any team structure, the person who presents the proposal is frequently not the person who will execute it. This is an ordinary consequence of division of labour rather than a flaw, but it means intent has to survive an internal handover, and the buyer should ask who executes.
  • Lock-in where assets are not transferred. If the code, the design source files or the platform account remain with the provider, moving elsewhere later means rebuilding. This is a contractual question, not an agency-specific one, but it has more consequence where a proprietary platform or internal framework is involved.
  • Slower cycles on small changes. Process that protects a large project can add latency to a two-hour amendment.

Risks of hiring an independent studio

  • Capacity is genuinely limited. This is the honest weakness of the model and it applies to Fritz directly. Only a small number of projects can run at once, and a start date can be several weeks out. A buyer with a fixed launch date should establish availability before anything else.
  • The same continuity exposure as a freelancer. One person means no internal cover, and an unplanned absence has no team to absorb it.
  • A narrower range than an agency. A studio that has defined its method has also defined what it does not take on. That is a feature when the project fits and a real limitation when it does not.
  • Scale ceiling. Work requiring several disciplines in parallel, sustained high volume, or a large internal stakeholder process is better served by a team.

Who each model actually suits

Matching the structure to the situation is more reliable than matching it to a budget. Each of the three has a set of conditions under which it is straightforwardly the right call.

A freelancer suits

A buyer who can write a clear brief and manage the work. Projects with a single dominant discipline, such as a front-end build against an existing design, a copywriting pass, or a set of layouts to be produced. Ongoing incremental work on an existing site where scope naturally arrives in small pieces. Buyers who already have internal capability and need extra hands rather than direction. If the brief is clear and the discipline is single, a freelancer is frequently the most efficient choice available, and paying for more structure would be paying for something the project does not use.

An agency suits

Organisations with several internal stakeholders who need a managed process to reach a decision. Projects spanning multiple disciplines that must run in parallel against a fixed date. Companies with compliance, procurement or reporting requirements that a single practitioner cannot satisfy. Long programmes where continuity across years matters more than the cost of coordination. Anyone who needs to know that the work continues when a specific individual is on holiday. When continuity and coverage are the priority, the structural cost is not overhead; it is the thing being bought.

An independent studio suits

Small and medium businesses, founders, and independent professionals with a defined deliverable, a real quality expectation, and no appetite for an open-ended process. Buyers who want the person doing the work to be the person they speak to. Situations where a fixed, known figure matters more than flexible scope. Projects sized as a single identity, a single site, or a single continuous stream of marketing work rather than several simultaneous workstreams.

Questions to ask any provider before signing

These five questions apply equally to a freelancer, an agency and a studio. They are more revealing than a portfolio review, because they surface structure rather than taste, and structure is what the earlier sections of this page showed to be the real variable. A good provider of any type answers all five without hesitation, and the answers can be compared across quotes that otherwise look nothing alike.

  • Who will actually execute the work. Ask for the name and the role of the person who will design and build, not the name of the company. If it is a team, ask who leads it and who the day-to-day contact will be. Distance between the sale and the build is manageable once it is known.
  • Whether the price is fixed, and fixed against what. Ask what is included, what is explicitly excluded, what triggers a variation, and what a variation costs. A fixed price attached to an undefined scope is not a fixed price.
  • Whether the code and the design source files are handed over at the end. Ask for it in writing, in what format, and whether the site can be hosted elsewhere without the provider's involvement. This single question does more to prevent lock-in than any other, and it is the point at which a one-person provider stops being a single point of failure: if the code, the design source files and the accounts are with the client, any competent provider can continue the work.
  • Who holds the domain, the hosting and the analytics accounts. These should be registered in the client's name, with the client as owner and the provider granted access. Retrospective transfers are possible but rarely pleasant.
  • What happens if the provider is unavailable. For a team, ask who covers. For an individual, ask what state the work would be left in and what the client would hold at that moment.

A sixth question is worth adding whenever a monthly figure appears: what the notice period is, and what the client keeps on leaving. A retainer that cannot be exited cleanly leaves a client paying for an arrangement after the work it was set up to cover has finished, so the exit terms deserve as much attention as the monthly figure itself.

How to reach a decision without a long procurement process

A useful sequence, in order, for a buyer who wants to decide within a week rather than a quarter. None of these steps requires a formal tender, and together they take less time than reading three proposals.

First, write down the deliverable in one sentence. If it can be written in one sentence, fixed pricing is available and an open-ended hourly arrangement is probably unnecessary. If it cannot, the scope is not yet defined, and the first purchase should be a short paid definition phase rather than a build. A definition phase bought deliberately is far cheaper than one discovered halfway through a project.

Second, count the disciplines the project genuinely requires in parallel. One or two points toward a freelancer or a studio. Four or five running simultaneously against a fixed date points toward an agency. Counting them in parallel rather than in total matters: a project that needs four disciplines in sequence over four months is a different problem from one that needs four in the same fortnight.

Third, decide how much internal management capacity is available. A freelancer usually needs a client who directs. An agency provides the direction and charges for it. A studio provides it without a separate coordination layer, at the cost of capacity. A buyer with no time to direct the work should not choose the model that assumes they will.

Fourth, ask the five questions above of every provider under consideration, and compare the answers rather than the totals. Two quotes that differ significantly usually differ because they finance different structures, not because one provider is better value than another.

Fifth, calibrate the figures against ranges observed on the Swiss market before treating any quote as high or low. That calibration is covered in detail on the cost guide, which is the right companion page to this one: this page decides which kind of provider to approach, and that page decides whether the figure they return is reasonable. For identity and logo work specifically, the same calibration is set out separately, since scope varies more there than on a website.
