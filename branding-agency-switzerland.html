Branding agency in Switzerland

Looking for a branding agency in Switzerland and working in English: this page explains what a brand identity actually contains, what the market charges for it, and the one constraint that shapes almost every Swiss brand, which is that it will be read in more than one language. It is written for founders and decision makers who are building here, often without a local reference for what any of this should cost.

A full brand identity in Switzerland is commonly quoted between CHF 1'200 and CHF 3'500 on the market, and at that level it should include a logo system, a colour palette, typography, written usage rules and the editable source files.

What a branding agency in Switzerland is actually selling

The phrase covers a very wide range of work. At one end, someone draws a mark and sends back a PNG. At the other, a consultancy runs a positioning programme across several months with research, naming and an internal rollout. Both are sold under the same two words, and the pages selling them rarely say which one is on offer. If you are new to the Swiss market, the first useful step is not choosing a studio, it is deciding which of three purchases you are making.

In the middle case, the one many companies need, what you are buying is a decision system. Every document, sign, invoice, deck, package and page you produce from now on will raise questions about size, colour, spacing, hierarchy and tone. An identity answers those questions once, in writing, so that nobody has to answer them again under deadline pressure, and so that the answer is the same whether the file is made by you, by a printer in Zurich or by a developer you hire next year.

That is why the deliverable is a system and a set of editable files rather than a single image. A mark you cannot rebuild, resize or hand to someone else is not an asset, it is a picture you are renting from whoever holds the source file.

A Swiss identity also has to survive a specific set of contexts before it is any good:

  • A wordmark that stays legible at the size of a business card and at the size of a shopfront
  • Documents that carry a legal or administrative weight: quotes, invoices, contracts, terms
  • Print produced by a supplier you will never speak to, working from your files
  • A website where the same page exists in two or three languages
  • Slides and PDFs made by people who are not designers, using the templates you give them

None of those contexts is exotic. They are simply the ordinary life of a small company that trades in Switzerland, and they are the reason an identity is judged by how it behaves at the edges rather than by how it looks in a presentation.

Logo, brand identity, branding: three different purchases

A logo

A logo is a single mark. Bought on its own, it usually arrives as a handful of exported files with little or no guidance on how to use it. It solves the immediate problem of having something to put on a card, and it postpones every other problem. It is a reasonable purchase if you genuinely only need a mark, and a poor one if you are about to produce a website, packaging or a document set, because the questions the logo does not answer will simply be answered inconsistently later.

A brand identity

An identity is the logo plus everything that makes it usable: variants of the mark for different formats, a colour palette with defined values for screen and print, a typographic system with sizes and weights that relate to each other, spacing and clear-space rules, and a short written document that says what to do and what not to do. It includes the editable source files. This is the level most small and mid-sized companies should buy, and it is what CHF 1'200 covers at Fritz.

A branding programme

A programme goes further upstream: positioning, naming, verbal identity, message architecture, sometimes research, then the identity itself and an extended set of applications. It is a longer engagement quoted per project, not from a list. It earns its cost when the strategic question is genuinely open, for example when a company is entering a new market or merging two entities. When the strategic question is already settled and the company knows what it is, the money is usually better spent on a competent identity and on the applications that carry it.

The multilingual reality of a Swiss brand

Switzerland is a multilingual country. French, German and Italian are all national languages, the language regions are geographic rather than blended, and English is widely used as a working language in international business. Geneva in particular has a large international community, which means a company can be headquartered here and conduct almost all of its internal life in English while its customers, suppliers and administration operate in French.

For a brand, that is not a translation problem. It is a design constraint that has to be handled at the point the system is built, because retrofitting it is expensive.

The same sentence sets at different lengths in different languages. German compounds run long and break awkwardly. French is generally longer than English. A lockup that sits neatly under a wordmark in English can wrap to two lines in French and three in German, and a navigation bar that fits in English can collapse in German. If the identity was only ever tested in one language, you find this out on the day the second language ships.

  • Descriptor lines under the logo: decide whether the descriptor translates, or whether it stays in one language everywhere
  • Navigation and button labels: size the components for the longest language, not the shortest
  • Typography: choose faces with complete accented characters and a weight range that survives long words
  • Document templates: quotes and invoices often need to exist in two languages with the same layout
  • Tone: an English brand voice does not automatically survive translation into French, and a literal translation usually sounds wrong

There is also a straightforward strategic question that most Swiss companies have to answer explicitly rather than by default: which languages the brand commits to. Committing to English and French is a real position. Committing to English only, with the understanding that a French version follows when the company crosses into the wider market, is also a real position. What causes damage is not choosing, and then improvising a second language into a system that was never designed to hold one.

The cheapest moment to answer that question is the briefing conversation. The most expensive moment is after the templates have been distributed to the people who use them every day.

Naming for a multilingual market: neutral or anchored

This is the decision an international founder in Switzerland faces most often, and it is where a naming choice made quickly costs the most later. Broadly there are two directions, and they are both legitimate.

The language-neutral name

An invented, abstract or Latin-rooted name belongs to no region in particular. It crosses the language border without changing meaning, it is usually easier to register and to secure a domain for, and it will not sound provincial to a customer in Zurich or a partner abroad. The cost is that it starts empty. It means nothing until you spend time and material making it mean something, which puts more weight on the identity and the writing around it.

The anchored name

A name rooted in a language, a place or a craft arrives with meaning already attached. It is warmer, more specific, and often more memorable in its home region. The cost is that the anchor may not travel: a name that reads as precise and local in French can read as foreign or simply opaque in German-speaking Switzerland, and vice versa. That is not automatically a problem. It is only a problem if your growth plan crosses the language border and the name was chosen as though it would not.

Practical checks before you commit

  • Say it out loud in French and in German. If the pronunciation shifts, decide whether you accept both or enforce one
  • Check for unintended meanings and awkward homophones in the languages you will actually operate in
  • Check the domain and the main social handles before falling in love with the name, not after
  • Decide whether the legal entity name and the trading name need to be the same
  • Have the trademark question checked by a trademark professional; that is a legal matter and not something a design studio should answer for you

Naming is not part of the CHF 1'200 identity. If you arrive without a name, that is a separate conversation and a separate quote, and the studio says so before any work starts rather than folding it silently into a design fee.

Precision over boldness: the Swiss design tradition

There is a design tradition here that predates all of this: grid systems, restrained typography, high standards of execution, and a general suspicion of decoration that does not do work. It shows up in signage, in public documents, in packaging, and in the visual habits of the market a Swiss company sells into.

The practical consequence for a company operating in Switzerland is that visible effort in the wrong place tends to read as a lack of confidence rather than as ambition. A logo that is trying hard, a palette with too many colours, a typeface chosen for personality rather than legibility: against that tradition they read as unresolved. Alignment that is nearly right, spacing that is inconsistent between two documents, a mark that changes proportion between the website and the invoice: if your audience is used to that standard of execution, these are the details that can quietly cost credibility, and nobody is likely to tell you they noticed.

This is not an argument for making everything grey and quiet. Restraint is not the absence of decisions, it is the result of having made them. A brand can be warm, specific and distinctive, and still hold a strict grid. What restraint rules out is arbitrariness, which is why the written rules matter more than they look like they should.

It also has a direct commercial consequence for anyone arriving from a market with looser norms: the expectation for execution quality is high, and meeting it is closer to table stakes than to differentiation. Distinctiveness has to come from a real idea about the company, not from turning up the volume on the graphics.

What an identity costs in Switzerland, and what CHF 1'200 means

Prices are rarely published here, which leaves a newcomer with little to calibrate a quote against. The useful reference point for this page is the identity range only: a full brand identity is commonly quoted between CHF 1'200 and CHF 3'500 on the market, covering a logo system, colour, typography, usage rules and the source files. Ranges for brochure sites, custom builds, e-commerce, ongoing maintenance and agency hourly rates are set out on the cost guide, which is the right place to read them because they belong to a different purchase.

Brand identity at Fritz is CHF 1'200, one-off. That is exactly at the bottom of the observed range for a full identity, at the floor rather than beneath it, and the distinction is worth making because pages that claim to be below the floor of every range are usually wrong about at least one. What keeps the number where it is has to do with the shape of the studio rather than with a promotion. Fritz is one person, so a brief reaches the person drawing the marks without passing through an account layer, and no time is billed to internal coordination or handover. The price is published in advance, so it does not move upward when a project looks like it could carry more, and the scope is stated before the work begins rather than discovered as it goes.

What that price does not buy is unlimited scope. It buys a defined identity, delivered properly. A naming programme, packaging artwork across a product range, photography, illustration systems or a multi-market rollout are real work and are quoted separately. Naming the boundary is more useful than a low headline figure that expands on contact.

One more thing worth knowing when comparing identity quotes: ask explicitly whether the editable source files are included. A quote that looks competitive can turn out to exclude them, which means every future change has to come back through the same supplier at whatever the rate is then. At Fritz the source files are handed over as part of the work, along with the code when a website is involved.

What CHF 1'200 buys, item by item

The identity is a fixed scope. This is what is delivered:

  • A primary logo and the variants the system actually needs: horizontal, stacked, a compact mark for small formats and for avatars, and a monochrome version
  • A colour palette with defined values for screen and for print, including which colours are primary, which are secondary and where each is allowed to be used
  • A typographic system: the faces, the weights, the sizes and the relationships between heading, subheading and body, so that hierarchy is a rule rather than a judgement call each time
  • Clear-space and minimum-size rules, so the mark is not crushed into a corner by someone in a hurry
  • Usage rules in plain language, including the misuses to avoid, written so that a non-designer can follow them
  • The editable source files, plus exports in the formats a printer, a developer and a social platform will each ask you for

It is deliberately a working document rather than a long presentation. The measure of an identity is not how impressive the deck is, it is whether the person who has to produce something on a Friday afternoon can find the answer in it.

If the identity is going onto a website, brand and site are better designed as one piece rather than handed from one supplier to another, and that combined engagement is covered on the brand and website page.

Working in English on a brand that will speak French

Plenty of companies here run their internal life in English and their external documents in French. That is a normal Swiss situation and it is not a contradiction, but it does put one requirement on the brand work: the process can be in English, and the artefacts have to be correct in the languages the company actually trades in.

In practice that means the briefing, the design reviews and the written usage document can all happen in English, while the templates, descriptors and document layouts are built and tested against French text lengths and French typographic conventions from the start. Fritz works in both, which is the reason this page exists in English and its counterpart exists in French rather than as a machine translation of each other.

It also means being explicit about a handful of small things that are easy to miss from outside the market: accented characters in every weight you intend to use, quotation and spacing conventions that differ from English usage, and the fact that an administrative document set is often the first thing a Swiss counterparty sees of your brand.

How the work runs, and the fourteen day timeline

An identity takes around fourteen days from the point the brief is agreed. The timeline is indicative and depends mostly on how quickly feedback comes back, not on how long the design takes.

  • Brief: one conversation about what the company does, who it serves, in which languages it operates and where the brand will physically appear. Language commitments are decided here, not later
  • Direction: a small number of distinct territories rather than a large number of variations on one idea, so the choice is a real choice
  • Development: the chosen direction built out into a full system, with the mark tested at the sizes and in the contexts it will actually face, including the languages you have committed to
  • Rules and handover: the usage document written, the files exported and organised, everything transferred to you

The terms are the same for every project and are not negotiated case by case. The prices are published, the source files are yours at the end, and the point of contact is the person doing the design. Messages get a reply within 24 hours, and a quote costs nothing.

A self-initiated identity concept for an artisan gelateria, and the piece of work that best shows what this level of engagement produces. The problem is the one most food and hospitality businesses have: the identity has to survive contexts that have nothing in common with each other. A shopfront read from across the street, a tub label read from thirty centimetres away, a menu board, a paper cup, a social post, an invoice.

That range is exactly what a system solves and a single logo does not. The mark needed a compact form for small formats and a full form for the front of the shop; the palette needed to hold up both in print on packaging and on a backlit screen; the typography needed to carry a product list without turning into decoration. The written rules matter more here than in most sectors, because the person applying the brand day to day is usually not a designer and is usually busy.

Frequently asked questions

How much does branding cost in Switzerland?

Ranges observed on the market put a full brand identity between CHF 1'200 and CHF 3'500, with wider branding programmes involving positioning, naming and rollout quoted well above that. A logo bought on its own generally costs less and delivers correspondingly less. Brand identity at Fritz is CHF 1'200 as a one-off payment, which sits at the bottom of that observed range, exactly at its floor. The ranges for websites, e-commerce and ongoing maintenance are set out on the cost guide.

What is the difference between a logo and a brand identity?

A logo is one mark. A brand identity is the system around it: variants of the mark for different formats, a colour palette defined for screen and print, a typographic system with a defined hierarchy, clear-space and minimum-size rules, written usage guidance and the editable source files. The practical test is what happens when someone who is not a designer has to produce a document or a label. With a logo alone they improvise. With an identity they follow a rule.

Does a Swiss brand need to be multilingual?

Not always, but it needs to decide. Switzerland has French, German and Italian as national languages and English is widely used as a business language, so a brand that crosses language regions usually needs at least French and German, while a company whose working language is English may reasonably start in English and add French later. The point is to design for the languages you commit to from the start, because a lockup, a navigation bar or a document template built and tested in one language often breaks when a longer language is added afterwards.

How long does a brand identity take?

Around fourteen days from the agreed brief, as an indicative timeline. The sequence is a briefing conversation, a small number of distinct directions, development of the chosen one into a full system tested at real sizes and in the relevant languages, then the written rules and the file handover. What moves the date is almost always the speed of feedback rather than the design work itself, so if several people need to approve, plan for that.

What files should I receive with my logo?

You should receive the editable source files, not just exports, along with vector formats for print and scaling, raster exports at the sizes screens and social platforms ask for, and a monochrome version. Ask about this explicitly before signing anything: a quote can look competitive and still exclude the source files, which means every future change has to go back through the same supplier. At Fritz the source files are handed over as part of the work, and the code as well when a website is involved.

Can the branding work be done entirely in English?

Yes. Briefing, direction, review and the written usage document are all available in English, and the studio also works in French, which is often what the finished brand has to speak. That combination is the point: an English-language process can still produce templates, descriptors and document layouts that are correct in French, provided the language commitments are made at the briefing stage rather than discovered at handover.
